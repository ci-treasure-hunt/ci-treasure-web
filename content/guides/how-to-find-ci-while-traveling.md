# How to Find Contact Improvisation While Traveling

Contact Improvisation is one of the easier things to find abroad once you know how to look, and
one of the harder ones if you look the way you'd look for anything else. There is no chain, no
franchise, and no central booking system. What there is instead is a few hundred local
communities, most of them run by volunteers, most of them organizing through a group chat.

This guide covers how to find them before you land, what to expect when you show up, and how to
time a trip around the events worth planning for.

## Why CI is harder to find than a yoga class

Most movement practices you can find abroad by searching for a studio. CI mostly doesn't work that
way. Jams are often held in rented halls one evening a week, organized by someone with a day job,
announced in a chat group, and invisible to search engines entirely. A city can have a healthy
weekly jam running for fifteen years and almost no web presence for it.

This is a direct consequence of how the form developed. CI has no governing body, no
certification, and no central organization. Nobody was ever in charge of making it findable.

So the practical answer is: don't search for venues. Find the community.

## Before you land

**Start with a directory.** [CI Treasure Hunt](/) maps {communityCount} CI communities across
{communityCountries} countries, plus upcoming festivals, jams, and teachers. Search the city you're going to, and if
nothing comes up, search the country. The nearest active scene is often one city over, and in
smaller countries it may be the only one.

**Then find the group chat.** This is the step people miss. A community listing gets you the
existence of a scene. The group chat gets you this week's actual jam, which is frequently
announced three days in advance and nowhere else.

**Message a human before you go.** CI communities are small and generally welcoming to visitors,
and a short message ("I'm visiting Lisbon 12 to 19 October, is the Tuesday jam running?") gets a
better answer than any website will. Organizers are used to this. It is not an imposition.

## Which app, in which country

CI communities do not all use the same messaging app, and the split is strongly regional. This is
the difference between finding a jam and not finding one: turn up with only Telegram installed and
you are locked out of most of Spain.

The lists below come from the invite links in CI Treasure Hunt's own community records. They say
which apps CI groups actually use in a country, not which app that country uses generally, and
those two aren't always the same thing.

- **WhatsApp** appears in more countries than anything else we hold: Spain, the UK, Ireland, the
  Netherlands, Belgium, Portugal, France, Germany, Austria, Italy, Greece, Finland, Israel, India,
  Nepal, Thailand, Vietnam, South Korea, Australia, the United States, and across Latin America in
  Brazil, Mexico, Argentina, Colombia, Costa Rica and Guatemala.
- **Telegram**: Germany, Switzerland, Spain, France, Portugal, Finland, Ukraine, Russia, Georgia,
  Kazakhstan, Egypt, India, Indonesia, Singapore, South Korea, Thailand, Australia and Uruguay.
- **Signal**: Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, France and Denmark. Some German communities run
  Signal and nothing else, so it's worth having if you're spending time there.
- **LINE**: Japan and Thailand.
- **WeChat**: mainland China, where WhatsApp has been blocked since 2017 and Telegram is
  unreliable. We hold no Chinese group chat on file, so this one is about the country rather than
  about our records.

Notice how many countries appear in more than one list. That's the useful part. Spain, Germany,
France, Portugal, Thailand, India and others run on two or three apps at once, and two groups in
the same city can be on different ones. Install more than one and search all of them before
concluding a place has nothing.

Two honest caveats, since this is our own data and not a survey. It reflects the invite links we
happen to have collected, and our coverage is uneven: we hold a lot for Spain and Germany and
almost none for the United States, so a country missing from these lists says more about us than
about it. Several entries rest on a single group, so treat all of this as a hint about what to
install, not as a measurement.

Install what the region uses before you arrive, not after. Some invite links are region-locked or
expire, which is easier to sort out while you still have reliable wifi.

## What to expect when you arrive

Formats vary more than newcomers expect. A jam might open with a facilitated warm-up or with
nothing at all. It might run in silence (this is normal in CI and not a sign something has gone
wrong) or with a musician. It might end with a closing circle.

Prices are usually low and often donation-based. In much of Europe expect somewhere between five
and fifteen euros for a weekly jam, sometimes a sliding scale, sometimes a bowl by the door.
Bring small cash. Card payment at a community jam is rare.

Etiquette is mostly universal, but not entirely. Norms around touch, talking during dances, and
how newcomers are welcomed do vary between communities and countries. The [jam etiquette
guide](/guides/contact-improvisation-jam-etiquette) covers the general case. When you arrive
somewhere new, the reliable move is to say you're visiting and ask how they do things.

**Language is rarely a barrier.** CI is mostly non-verbal. Warm-up instructions are often
translated for visitors, and if they aren't, saying so usually fixes it.

## What to bring

- Something with covered legs and shoulders, for the jams that expect it and for floor work
  generally, since skin against skin grabs and drags. Plenty of jams are full of shorts and vest
  tops and nobody minds, so this is about having the option, not a dress code. The [jam etiquette
  guide](/guides/contact-improvisation-jam-etiquette) covers how much this varies.
- Nothing with zips, buckles, belts, or hard buttons.
- No jewelry. Rings and earrings are the usual injury culprits.
- Kneepads if you already use them, though many jams have spares.
- Small cash, as above.
- Socks, for colder floors. Otherwise CI is danced barefoot.

## Timing a trip around events

Multi-day events cluster seasonally, and they are worth planning around instead of stumbling into.
The European [festival](/festivals) season runs roughly April to October. July and August are the peak, and
October is busier than people expect, close to level with high summer. The larger festivals sell
out months ahead. Southeast Asia runs on
the opposite cycle: the Thailand and wider Asia season falls in the northern winter, roughly
November to March, when the weather works and the European scene is quiet.

If you're traveling long-term, this pairs well: Europe in summer, Asia or South America in
winter. A lot of the CI dancers you meet doing exactly this are following that loop.

For a first festival specifically, the [festival prep
guide](/guides/first-ci-festival-prep) covers what those weeks actually look like.

## Staying longer

If you're somewhere for more than a couple of weeks, going deeper than the weekly jam is usually
possible and rarely advertised. Ask locally about regular classes, ongoing training groups,
residencies, and labs. Communities frequently have things running that never make it onto any
public listing, for the same reason the jams don't: nobody thought to publish it.

This is also how you meet people properly. Turning up to the same jam three weeks running changes
how a community relates to you far more than any single visit does.

If you end up staying for good, [finding CI where you live](/guides/how-to-find-ci-where-you-live)
covers building an ongoing practice, including what to do when you land somewhere with no scene at
all.
