# Preparing for Your First Contact Improvisation Festival

A CI festival is a different animal from a weekly jam. It's several days of concentrated practice,
usually with visiting teachers and mostly with people you've never met, and it tends to be more
physically and socially demanding than anything a local scene prepares you for. None of that is a
reason to skip it. It just helps to know what you're walking into.

Every upcoming one we know of is listed under [festivals](/festivals), with
[intensives](/intensives), [retreats](/retreats), [workshops](/workshops) and
[long jams](/long-jams) alongside. If you haven't found one yet, the [travel
guide](/guides/how-to-find-ci-while-traveling)
covers that. This one assumes you've already registered for something.

## What does a day at a CI festival look like?

Structures vary, but a shape repeats across most CI festivals: mornings tend to start quietly,
often with an optional early practice before breakfast (sitting meditation, [Contemplative Dance
Practice](/guides/glossary#contemplative-dance-practice-cdp), or gentle solo movement). A longer
mid-morning block follows, usually a guided warm-up, a class, or open jamming. Afternoons hold classes, lab time, rest, or a walk.
Evenings usually close with an open jam.

That's a lot of structured time, and it's worth knowing going in that you are not expected to fill
every slot. Skipping a session to rest is normal and doesn't need explaining to anyone.

## How long do CI festivals run?

Anywhere from a long weekend, three or four days, to a full week or more. Check your specific
event instead of assuming: a first-timer's four-day festival and someone else's ten-day intensive
are both called "a CI festival" and prepare you for very different commitments.

## Where will you sleep, and what does it cost?

Most festivals offer a few tiers side by side: camping, a shared sleeping hall, dorms, and private
rooms, at different price points. Camping is often the cheapest and is common even at events that
also offer indoor housing.

Check what's included before you book. A camping ticket usually means you bring the tent, the mat
and the bedding yourself. Dorms are commonly bunks for six to eight people. Private rooms are the
expensive tier and the one that sells out first.

Pricing is frequently sliding scale, meaning you're asked to pay what you can within a stated range
instead of a single fixed number.

Sliding scale isn't a sign the event is struggling financially. It's a deliberate choice to keep
the door open to people at different income levels, and the honest thing is to pay what you
actually can instead of defaulting to the bottom of the band.

## Can you do a festival cheaply?

Often yes, if you plan early.

**Sliding scale** is the first lever and it's built in. Pay what you honestly can within the band.

**Work exchange** is the second. Many festivals offer a small number of places at a reduced or
waived fee in return for a few hours of help a day: kitchen, setup, cleaning, the registration
desk. The catch is supply. Spots are limited, often a handful per event, and they usually open when
registration opens and fill fast. If work exchange is how you're planning to afford the festival,
apply the day registration opens, not the month before the event.

**Camping** is usually the cheapest accommodation tier by a wide margin.

Some festivals also hold lower sliding-scale places for people from groups underrepresented at that
particular event. If a festival does this, its pricing page will say so.

## What to pack for a CI festival

Start with what any jam needs, covered in the [travel
guide](/guides/how-to-find-ci-while-traveling): clothes you can move in, no jewelry, small cash. A
multi-day stay adds a lot on top of that, and what it adds depends on where you're sleeping and
what the climate is.

### The CI-specific things

- **Far more dance clothes than feels reasonable.** You'll sweat through one or two sets a day and
  laundry access varies. More than you would pack for any other kind of week away, and in a hot
  hall people change mid-jam, so a set per session is not excessive. Long sleeves and long trousers
  at minimum, plus lighter options for warm halls.
- **A bar of laundry soap.** Sink washing is how most people get through a week, and it's the item
  people most often forget.
- **Kneepads**, if you use them. Commonly encouraged, rarely required, and some festivals have
  spares.
- **Socks**, including grippy dance socks if you have them, for cold floors.
- **A refillable water bottle.**

### If you're camping

- **Tent, sleeping mat, sleeping bag.** A camping ticket almost always means you supply all three.
  Confirm instead of assuming; a few festivals provide tents.
- **A head torch**, and something warm for the tent that isn't your dance clothes.

### If you're in a dorm or sleeping hall

- **Earplugs and an eye mask.** In a large sleeping hall people come and go all night, heading off
  to dance or coming back from it, going to the toilet, arriving at three because the jam finally
  wore them out. Nobody is being inconsiderate; that's just what a shared hall is.
- **A small torch**, so you can find your things at night without lighting the whole room.
- **A bag you can live out of.** Floor space is shared and gets chaotic fast.

### If the venue is cold

Anywhere outside high summer, and plenty of places in it. Halls get heated for moving bodies, not
for sitting still.

- Layers for the gaps between sessions, and something dry to change into after one.
- Warm socks, and a hat if the site involves walking outdoors between buildings.

### If the venue is tropical

Asia, Latin America, and the warmer end of the European summer.

- Light long sleeves and long trousers anyway. Covered skin is about sliding contact, not warmth.
- Insect repellent, a mosquito net if you're camping, and rehydration salts.
- Even more clothes than you packed for a temperate venue. You'll go through them faster.

### Also worth having

- **Something to eat between meals**, if you tend to run low on energy. Kitchens usually cook
  around the schedule, but a full day of dancing burns through more than three meals allow for.
- **A journal**, if you're someone who processes that way. Multi-day intensives surface a lot,
  physically and emotionally.
- **Read what the festival sends you.** Organizers usually email a packing list and practical
  details beforehand, sometimes with more on the event website, and theirs will be more specific
  about that venue than this one can be.

## How to pace yourself across several days

This is the thing a single evening jam never teaches you, and it's the most common reason people
have a rough time at their first festival: **going full intensity on day one and running out of
capacity by day three.**

A few things that help. Rest is not optional at a multi-day event; sitting out a session, or a
whole day, is a completely normal and common choice, not a failure to commit. Pay attention to
your own signals instead of the room's energy; a festival gathers people at very different
experience levels, and the fact that someone next to you is going hard doesn't mean you should.
Eating and sleeping enough sounds obvious and is the first thing that slips when a schedule is
this full.

## The social side

You'll be around the same people for days, anywhere from a few dozen at a small gathering to
several hundred at a large one, which is a different situation from a jam where you might not see
anyone again for a week. Two things follow that people don't expect.

Relationships compress. You'll have danced with someone for twenty minutes before you know their
name or what country they live in, and that produces a closeness running well ahead of the ordinary
getting-to-know-you. It's one of the genuinely good things about festivals, and it can also feel
disorienting around day two.

And there's nowhere to go. Constant contact with limited alone time is the standard festival
fatigue, except that here the contact is physical as well as social.

Finding your own rhythm inside the group schedule matters more than it sounds. Stepping away from a
meal, sitting out an evening jam, going for a walk on your own, leaving a conversation early: all
normal, all done constantly by people on their tenth festival, and none of it reads as antisocial.

**Consent and etiquette work the same as at any jam**, just for longer and with less time to reset
between dances. The [jam etiquette guide](/guides/contact-improvisation-jam-etiquette) still
applies in full; nothing about a festival setting suspends it.

## Sharing circles, and the part nobody warns you about

Several days of close physical contact, little sleep and no ordinary distractions will bring things
up. People find old patterns showing up in how they dance, or in who they avoid. Grief arrives
without an obvious cause. A dance goes somewhere unexpectedly tender and you don't know what to do
with it afterwards. Being briefly overwhelmed at a festival is common, and it isn't a sign you've
done anything wrong or picked the wrong event.

This is what **sharing circles** are for, and many festivals run them: a facilitated round, often at
the start and end and sometimes daily, where people say where they are and are listened to without
anyone fixing it. Some events run smaller standing groups instead, the same handful of people
meeting each day, so that by day four you're talking to faces you know. Names vary, opening and
closing circles, sharing rounds, sharing groups, and they show up on festival programs across
Europe and the Americas alike.

Go to them, particularly at your first festival. They're the community's own answer to the fact
that this practice asks a lot of people, and they're much easier to walk into on day one than on
day four when you actually need one. If your festival doesn't run any, the people to find are the
organizers or the Consent Team, and a conversation with one teacher is worth more than sitting with
it alone.

## If it's genuinely your first one

Tell people. Just as at a single jam, saying you're new gets you real accommodation from
experienced dancers and teachers, and it's the fastest way to find out how this particular event
actually runs.

Pick a shorter or smaller festival for your first one if you have the choice. A four-day event
with fifty people is an easier place to learn your own limits than a ten-day event with three
hundred.

Go in without a plan to attend everything. The people who have the best first festivals are
usually the ones who treat the schedule as a menu, not a checklist.
