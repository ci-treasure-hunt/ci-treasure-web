# Contact Improvisation Glossary

A reference for the terms that come up regularly in Contact Improvisation (CI) classes, jams, and
conversation. New to CI? Start with [What Is Contact
Improvisation?](/guides/what-is-contact-improvisation). This page is built to be looked up
term by term, not read start to finish.

## Principles and movement concepts

### Backspace

The space behind you, out of sight. CI pays deliberate attention to it. Lifts often happen onto or
from the back, dancers move back to back, and a lot of a duet happens outside what either person
can see. Orienting toward the backspace instead of always leading forward changes what you sense.
It asks for more listening and less prediction.

### Center-to-center

Contact between the dancers' physical centers, the lower belly, sometimes called the *hara*,
roughly where the body's center of mass sits. Bringing two centers close means one skeleton can
carry the other, which is what makes weight transfer and lifting possible without muscling. Contact
through limbs and surfaces alone leaves the weight out at arm's length, where it has to be pushed
rather than carried.

### Counterbalance

Two dancers leaning away from each other while staying connected at a shared point, each person's
weight balancing the other's. The stability comes from the opposition itself. Neither dancer could
hold the shape alone. Often used early in teaching, because it's real weight-sharing that doesn't
require lifts or floor work.

### Falling

Going with gravity instead of bracing against it, and treating a fall as movement to travel
through, not an accident to prevent. CI borrows heavily here from Aikido, where practitioners spend
years learning to drop out of a loss of balance and roll back up without injury. Paxton trained in
Aikido from 1964, and it shows in how CI teaches falling. This is one of the few things in CI
genuinely worth learning in a class rather than picking up at a jam.

### Listening

Attending through touch, weight, and peripheral senses to what a partner is actually doing and
offering, not imposing a plan. CI gets described as a listening practice as much as a moving one.

### Momentum

Using energy already in motion instead of initiating every action muscularly. Much of what looks
effortless or acrobatic in CI is momentum being redirected, not strength being applied.

### Negative space

Dancing where the other body *isn't*, moving in relation to a partner through the empty space
around them rather than through contact. A CI duet doesn't have to be touching continuously.
Negative space is the part of the dance that happens out of contact, and it's as much a part of
the form as weight-sharing.

### Off-balance

Moving through and from imbalance, not toward a stable center. Falling and catching both happen
from here. CI treats off-balance as material to work with, not something to avoid.

### Point of contact

The place where two dancers' bodies are touching. It rarely stays fixed. It travels across the
dancers' bodies by **rolling** (the contact area itself turning over), **sliding** (two surfaces
moving against each other without losing touch), or **pivoting** (rotating around a fixed point).
Following that travel instead of planning it is close to the physical core of what CI is.

### Reaching

Extending into space or toward a partner, through a limb or through the whole body. It does two
jobs. Reaching into space gives you something to move from: the extended limb becomes a support,
and orienting outward lets you find strength without gripping. At the point of contact, both
dancers are usually reaching gently toward each other, and that mutual reach is what keeps the
contact alive. Change the direction of your reaching and the contact changes or ends with it.
Reaching matters in lifts too, where a body that extends is easier to support than one that
collapses.

### Release

Letting go of unnecessary muscular holding so weight can travel through the skeleton into the
floor or a partner. Connected to the release-technique lineage in postmodern dance. It underlies
most of the other principles here, because weight can't be shared cleanly through a tense body.

### Score

A set of rules, structures, or constraints that shape an improvisation, from a single instruction
to a multi-hour form like the Underscore. It sets the conditions an improvisation happens within.
The movements themselves stay open, which is what separates a score from choreography.

### Small dance

An exercise originated by Steve Paxton: standing still and noticing the tiny, constant postural
adjustments the body makes to stay upright, the barely visible dance of balance itself. Used as a
way into sensing weight and structure before adding motion or a partner.

### Spirals

Rotational, helical pathways through the body. A primary way dancers travel into and out of the
floor, and around a partner, without stopping and restarting the movement.

### Weight-sharing

Giving physical weight to a partner, by leaning, falling, being caught, lifting or being lifted.
Touch alone isn't weight-sharing; the weight has to actually transfer. This is the defining
physical premise of CI. The two sides of it are *giving weight*, committing real weight into the
contact, and *taking weight* or *receiving weight*, supporting a partner's weight, ideally through
skeletal structure and not muscular effort. Both trade back and forth continuously.

### Witnessing

Watching from the edge of a jam with real attention, as opposed to simply sitting out. It's treated
as a way of taking part. CI shares the term with [Authentic Movement](#authentic-movement), where
the mover/witness pair is the basic structure, and CI labs and scores sometimes use it in that more
formal sense. At an ordinary jam it's informal: when the room's attention gathers around one duet,
the people watching are witnessing it. See the [jam etiquette
guide](/guides/contact-improvisation-jam-etiquette).

### Yielding

Meeting an incoming force, usually gravity, but also a partner's weight or push, by moving with it
gradually and smoothly rather than resisting or collapsing. The term comes from Bonnie Bainbridge
Cohen's developmental movement work (see [Body-Mind Centering](#body-mind-centering-bmc)), where
yielding to gravity is described as the body's very first movement. It isn't passive. Done gradually enough, the movement also counteracts the force
it's responding to, and it stays readable to a partner, who can sense it and respond. Related to
*release* and to giving weight, but describes the quality of the response itself, not the
letting-go of tension or the weight being handed over.

## Formats and gatherings

### Contemplative Dance Practice (CDP)

A movement meditation practice developed by Barbara Dilley, who taught it from around 1980 at the
Naropa Institute in Boulder, Colorado (renamed Naropa University in 1999). Dilley also danced in
the June 1972 group that named Contact Improvisation, which is part of why the two practices keep
turning up at the same events. A session moves through sitting meditation, then a personal
awareness practice, then a group practice called **Open Space**, closing with discussion. Different from CI in lineage and intent,
coming from Buddhist contemplative practice rather than postmodern dance research, though the two
overlap in many communities and often share practitioners and venues.

### Festival

A larger multi-day gathering combining classes, jams, and often performances, usually drawing
dancers from well beyond the local community.

### Intensive

A multi-day or multi-week period of concentrated CI training, usually with a smaller and more
consistent group than a festival. Depth over breadth.

### Jam

An open, informal gathering where anyone present can dance with anyone else, usually with a
warm-up period before open dancing begins. Typically leaderless, with no central instructor
guiding the session. The most common entry point into a CI community.

### Lab

Also called a research session. A more exploratory, less performance-oriented gathering focused on one physical question or theme.
Often used by experienced practitioners, or by teachers developing new material.

### Silent jam

A jam held without music. Common in CI, and a deliberate choice rather than an absence. Music
tends to unify a room's mood and rhythm, and many hosts would rather dancers follow physical
sensation and each other. CI doesn't follow music the way most social dances do.

### Underscore

A long-form structured jam developed by Nancy Stark Smith, moving participants through a sequence
of named phases over several hours: arriving, small dance, solo movement, entering duets, resting,
group sections, and more. It makes the usually invisible phases of a jam explicit and shared.

## Terms from adjacent practices

### Authentic Movement

A movement practice built on a pair of roles: a *mover*, who moves with eyes closed following inner
impulse, and a *witness*, who watches without directing. It grew out of Mary Starks Whitehouse's
work, which she called Movement-in-depth, and was developed into the mover/witness discipline
largely by Janet Adler. Separate from CI in lineage and intent, though the two overlap in
practitioners and venues, and CI borrows the term *witnessing* from it (see
[Witnessing](#witnessing)).

### Axis Syllabus

A reference system for human movement assembled by Frey Faust, drawing on anatomy, biomechanics and
physics, not on any one dance style. Faust is a second-generation contact improviser out of
the 1980s New York scene, which is part of why the two circles overlap so heavily: Axis Syllabus
classes are a common sight on CI festival programs, and a lot of CI teachers have studied it. It
is not a dance form and not a style of CI. It's an analytical framework for how bodies move, used
alongside CI, not instead of it.

### Body-Mind Centering (BMC)

An approach to movement and body awareness founded by Bonnie Bainbridge Cohen, studying the body
through its systems and through the developmental movement patterns infants pass through. Its
vocabulary has fed into CI teaching, most visibly in the idea of *yielding* (see
[Yielding](#yielding)). Like Authentic Movement, it's a distinct practice with its own training,
not a branch of CI.

### Conscious dance

An umbrella term sometimes used for movement practices oriented around self-inquiry, embodiment, or
personal process rather than performance or technique, covering practices like 5Rhythms, Ecstatic
Dance, and Open Floor (see the [comparison
guide](/guides/contact-improvisation-vs-other-practices)). Some festivals draw dancers from both
conscious dance and CI communities. Whether CI itself counts as part of that category varies by
community and isn't settled; this glossary doesn't take a position on it.

### Material for the Spine

Steve Paxton's own long-running movement research, developed after CI and studied by many CI
teachers. It's solo work, not partner work, focused on the spine and pelvis and on fine internal
sensation. Usually described as a companion practice to CI, not a part of it.

### Partnering

A general term, used across ballet, contemporary dance, and ballroom, for the technique of moving
in weighted contact with another dancer. CI is built entirely around partnering and continuous
weight-sharing, but partnering itself is a broader skill CI shares with many other dance forms,
not something unique to it.

### Somatic movement

An umbrella term for practices that work from internal sensation, attention and the felt experience
of moving, instead of from an external shape to be achieved. It covers a wide and loosely bounded
field, [Body-Mind Centering](#body-mind-centering-bmc), Feldenkrais, Alexander Technique,
Continuum and others among them. It's the label that turns up most often alongside CI in event
listings, and many CI teachers hold a training in one somatic practice or another. CI itself is
usually described as somatically informed, not as a somatic practice in its own right,
though where the line falls depends who you ask.
